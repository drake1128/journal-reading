CLINICAL CASE
Radiation-Induced Cardiac Disease
Dilemma of Transcatheter Aortic Valve Replacement vs
Surgical Aortic Valve Replacement and Pericardiectomy
Astefanos Al-Dalakta, MD, Joseph El Roumi, MD, Gary Parizher, MD, Chadi Tabaja, MD, Issam Motairek, MD,
Jibran Ikram, MD, Muhammad Ehsan, MD, Allan L. Klein, MD
ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND Radiation-induced cardiovascular disease, a complication of mediastinal radiation, often results in
valvular and pericardial disease. Management is challenging, especially in high-risk patients.
CASE SUMMARY A 59-year-old man with a history of total body radiation presented with fatigue and dyspnea. Mul-
timodality imaging, including echocardiography, cardiac magnetic resonance, and invasive hemodynamic assessment,
conﬁrmed severe aortic stenosis and mild constrictive physiology. Because of an elevated surgical risk and minimum
contribution of constrictive physiology to symptoms, the multidisciplinary team opted for transcatheter aortic valve
replacement over surgical aortic valve replacement with pericardiectomy.
DISCUSSION This case underscores the complexities of radiation-induced cardiac disease management, emphasizing
the role of multimodality imaging in treatment planning. It highlights transcatheter aortic valve replacement as a viable
option for high-risk patients with aortic stenosis in this population.

MANAGEMENT
Given the patient’s presentation, a multidisciplinary
discussion balanced the risks and beneﬁts of 2 main
options: surgical aortic valve replacement (SAVR)
with pericardiectomy vs transcatheter aortic valve
replacement (TAVR) and close monitoring. SAVR
could
address
the
severe
AS,
whereas
peri-
cardiectomy could relieve the constrictive physi-
ology. However, this patient was considered high risk
for surgery because prior radiation exposure raises
perioperative mortality especially in the presence of
constrictive
pericarditis.
The
patient’s
history
of
recurrent pleural effusions and trapped lung also
suggested
radiation-induced
lung
damage,
which
could lead to a higher risk of respiratory complica-
tions. Additionally, the pericardial constriction was
mild to moderate, and its contribution to symptoms
remained uncertain. For the aforementioned reasons,
the decision was made to pursue TAVR. The plan
included close postprocedural monitoring for symp-
tom progression. If constrictive symptoms worsened,
pericardiectomy could be revisited as a secondary
intervention in the future.
OUTCOME AND FOLLOW-UP
The patient is being evaluated for a TAVR. He remains
under close follow-up with cardiology and cardiac
surgery teams to monitor his symptoms and assess for
disease progression.

Radiation-induced
cardiovascular
disease
is
well
documented in the medical literature but remains
under-recognized
in
clinical
practice.
Radiation
therapy continues to play a critical role in cancer
treatment, despite its associated risks. In carefully
selected cases, the therapeutic beneﬁts are consid-
ered to outweigh the potential complications. How-
ever, high doses of mediastinal radiation can cause
damage to nearly all cardiac structures, including the

Thoracic radiation has been consistently linked to
an increased risk of valvular heart disease. The prev-
alence
of
radiation-induced valvulopathy
reaches
approximately 26% within 10 years and rises to 60% at
20 years post-treatment.1 The median time from can-

23 years, with the prevalence and severity of the
condition closely correlated with the cumulative ra-
diation dose received.2 Percutaneous approaches for
managing valvulopathy in patients with prior medi-
astinal
radiation
are
gaining
attention,
as
they
circumvent many surgical challenges associated with
radiation-induced
chest
complications.
Although
aortic regurgitation is the most frequently observed
abnormality, AS frequently necessitates intervention.
Data are mixed on the best approach to AS in such
populations.3
Only
a
single
study
has
directly
compared outcomes between TAVR and SAVR in pa-
tients with severe AS and a history of mediastinal ra-
diation. The retrospective analysis, conducted at the
Mayo Clinic from 2011 to 2018, found that TAVR was
associated with shorter hospital stays, lower post-
operative atrial ﬁbrillation rates, and reduced 30-day
mortality (1.8% vs 9.1%). However, TAVR patients
experienced higher readmission rates, predominantly
because of heart failure.4
Moreover, radiation exposure can result in various
pericardial
disorders,
ranging
from
asymptomatic
calciﬁcations and thickening to severe conditions such
as chronic constrictive pericarditis, acute pericarditis,
and cardiac tamponade. Over time, radiation-induced
changes replace normal pericardial adipose tissue
with collagen and ﬁbrin, leading to ﬁbrosis. These
changes may impair venous drainage and cause ﬁbri-
nous exudate accumulation, resulting in pericardial
effusion. Long-term effects, including calciﬁcation,
thickening, and stiffening of the pericardium, often
progress
to
constrictive
pericarditis,
typically
emerging decades after radiation exposure.5 Deﬁni-
tive treatment for constrictive pericarditis involves
radical
pericardiectomy,
although
prior
radiation
therapy is a signiﬁcant predictor of adverse outcomes
in
these
patients.
Radiation-induced
constrictive
pericarditis has the poorest survival among all etiol-
ogies, with a 5-year survival rate of only 12%.6 As a
result, the decision to proceed with surgery must
carefully consider the substantial procedural risks,
and in some cases, continued symptomatic manage-
ment of heart failure may be more appropriate.
In our case, the management strategy was guided
by the patient’s high surgical risk, mild to moderate
severity of pericardial constriction, and critical role of
cardiac multimodality imaging. Comprehensive im-
aging was essential in quantifying the extent of
pericardial
constriction
and
the
severity
of
AS,
providing a detailed assessment that informed the
decision-making process. A multidisciplinary team
carefully evaluated the risks and beneﬁts of SAVR
with pericardiectomy vs TAVR with close monitoring.
Although SAVR could address the severe AS and
pericardiectomy
could
alleviate
the
constrictive
physiology,
the
patient’s
elevated
surgical
risk,
coupled with the mild constriction and uncertainty
regarding its clinical impact, led to the selection of
TAVR and monitoring.
CONCLUSIONS
This case highlights the challenges in managing
radiation-induced
cardiovascular
disease,
particu-
larly
in
patients
with
severe
AS
and
coexisting
pericardial constriction.7 The use of cardiac multi-
modality imaging was critical for accurately assessing
the severity of both conditions and guiding treatment
decisions.
