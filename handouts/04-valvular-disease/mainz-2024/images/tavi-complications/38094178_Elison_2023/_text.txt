ABSTRACT
We present a series of 2 high-risk surgical patients undergoing transcatheter aortic valve replacement via alternative
access owing to unacceptable transfemoral options. Both patients underwent percutaneous transcarotid access with
successful valve delivery and deployment without notable complication. To our knowledge, this approach has not been
previously reported. (Level of Difﬁculty: Intermediate.) (J Am Coll Cardiol Case Rep 2023;26:102069)

INVESTIGATIONS. TTE revealed a heavily calciﬁed,
trileaﬂet aortic valve with severe AS (peak velocity
4.2 m/s, mean gradient 42 mm Hg, AVA 0.81 cm2) with
preserved left and right ventricular function. Coro-
nary angiography revealed no signiﬁcant coronary
disease. CTA demonstrated severely calciﬁed disease
at the bifurcation of the abdominal aorta (Figure 4A).
On upper extremity reconstruction, the right and left
axillary arteries also were inhospitable (Figure 4B).
MANAGEMENT. Similar to patient 1, given signiﬁ-
cantly elevated surgical risk, patient 2 was brought
forth for TAVR. As with patient 1, under general
anesthesia a single 6-F LFA access was obtained and
abdominal aortic angiography conﬁrmed severe ste-
nosis above the bifurcation. Left carotid angiography
was performed (Figure 5A), demonstrating a widely
patent common carotid with mild branch carotid
disease. The procedure was then completed similarly
as described for patient 1.
After 14-F sheath removal, the arteriotomy was
again closed with Perclose sutures, and ﬁnal angiog-
raphy conﬁrmed no extravasation or new stenosis in
the common or branch carotids (Figure 5B). She was
discharged on postoperative day 3 after an uncom-
plicated hospital course. Similarly to patient 1, she
demonstrated no neurologic deﬁcits.
Both patients were seen in clinic for 1-month
follow-up visits and remained free from neurologic
complaints.
DISCUSSION
The presented 2-patient case series represents, to our
knowledge, the ﬁrst published cases of percutaneous
transcarotid TAVR in prohibitively high-risk surgical
patients
requiring
alternative
access.
While
the
transfemoral (TF) approach to TAVR remains by far
the most common (>95% of procedures), in 2019
alone, 3,247 patients required alternative access in
some form, reﬂecting the relative frequency of the
problem, with the most frequent alternative access
sites
including
subclavian
(n
¼
1,100),
axillary
(n ¼ 706), carotid (n ¼ 662), and transaortic (n ¼ 341).1
The transcarotid (TC) approach, traditionally per-
formed via surgical cutdown, is a common second
choice in many centers for patients with prohibitive
femoral anatomy. Since the ﬁrst successful procedure
by Modine et al in 2010,2 the approach has gained
favor based on reassuring safety and efﬁcacy out-
comes compared with routine TF TAVR and other
alternative access sites. Contemporary data from
Chamandi et al examining 329 alternative-access
TAVR patients comparing the TC approach (n ¼ 101)

Stroke

demonstrate that the rate of in-hospital and 30-day

and not markedly different from those undergoing
a
TF

Some
have
advocated
for
extensive pre- and intra-procedural assessment and
monitoring, including imaging for patency of the
circle of Willis, cerebral saturation monitoring, and
carotid
stump
pressure
measurements
after
cross-clamp. However, in a single-center analysis,
Paone
et

published
similar
outcomes
for
TF
and TC TAVR using none of the aforementioned
monitoring,
suggesting
that
they
may
be
unnecessary.4
In our opinion, the present report represents the
next step in the natural progression of TC alternative
access. As necessity is often the mother of invention,
a percutaneous TC approach was considered in pa-
tient 1 owing to a total lack of other options. With
previous bilateral CEA, reoperation to facilitate access
was not desirable, and, as such, percutaneous access
was undertaken. Patient 2, of similar risk factor pro-
ﬁle without alternative options, underwent a percu-
taneous approach after an extensive discussion of
beneﬁt and risk. In TF TAVR, with greater technology
and operator experience, earlier practices such as
femoral cutdown have become increasingly obsolete.
Even in the early TAVR experience when 24- and 26-F
sheaths were required, comparisons of percutaneous
and surgical access of the femoral artery suggested
equivalency with more minor vascular complications
in the surgical exposure cohort.5 A comparison of
percutaneous and surgical access to the axillary ar-
tery has also demonstrated equivalent rates of death,
stroke, and bleeding but longer hospital length of stay
and intensive care unit utilization in the surgical
exposure arm.6 Thus, it is plausible that surgical
exposure for TC TAVR may not be as essential as
originally considered. Although the current report
highlights 2 cases performed under general anes-
thesia, with increasing operator comfort, general
anesthesia could be avoided. Furthermore, facility
with percutaneous carotid access may decrease pro-
cedure time, given the inherent efﬁciencies compared
with surgical cutdown, and could broaden access
options to centers with little surgical experience in
carotid procedures. Finally, percutaneous access ob-
viates the need for wound management and dressing
care, and could thereby lessen risks such as surgical
site infection. What remains unknown are the long-
term effects of common carotid percutaneous access
and endovascular closure, and how they may affect
outcomes.
Also novel here is the use of a balloon wedge
catheter inﬂated distal to the percutaneous carotid
access
site
to
minimize
cerebral
debris.

infrequently
encountered
in
the
interventional
cardiology
space,

idea
mimics
the

of

embolectomy
catheters,

broadly in vascular surgery. With patency of the
bilateral carotids demonstrated on pre-TAVR CTA,
and a presumptively intact circle of Willis, there
should be no increased risk to the patient from
balloon occlusion of the accessed common carotid for
a matter of minutes, especially in the setting of
managed hypertension. Following access site closure,
purposeful withdrawal of the inﬂated balloon (at low
pressure)
liberates
any
potential
debris
into
the
thoracic aorta, where it may scatter to less critical
tissue beds. This added layer of protection serves to
lessen the potential risk of periprocedural TIA/stroke
and does not appear to damage the vasculature or the
access point.
Overall, though a small sample, these initial cases
demonstrate that percutaneous TC TAVR can be per-
formed safely and successfully, and further study is
required before more broad generalization of the
approach.
