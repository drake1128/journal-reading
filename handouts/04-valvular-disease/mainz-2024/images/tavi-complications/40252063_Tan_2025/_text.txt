One-Stop TEVAR and TAVR Procedure
for Severe Aortic Stenosis With
Aortic Arch Aneurysm

Jun Zhang, MD, PHDa,b
ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND In recent years, transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) has become the fundamental treatment
for patients with aortic stenosis (AS). Thoracic endovascular aortic repair (TEVAR) is also recommended for penetrating
aortic ulcer. Especially in older patients with severe AS and penetrating aortic ulcer at the same time, the surgical method
brings signiﬁcant risk.
CASE SUMMARY We report a case of a 74-year-old male patient who presented with a 10-day history of dyspnea.
He was diagnosed with severe AS with aortic valve insufﬁciency (penetrating aortic ulcer) and had multiple comorbidities.
The patient underwent a 1-stop TEVAR þ TAVR procedure.
DISCUSSION The challenge was compounded by the patient’s extremely narrow femoral access. This case demon-
strates the 2 operations can be performed as a procedure.
TAKE-HOME MESSAGES Management of patients with severe AS and concurrent aortic pathology is complicated.
One-stop TEVAR þ TAVR procedure is viable and beneﬁcial in such patients. (JACC Case Rep. 2025;30:103922)

PAST MEDICAL HISTORY
The patient had a history of hypertension,
diabetes mellitus, and a coronary stent im-
plantation 12 years ago.
DIFFERENTIAL DIAGNOSIS
Echocardiography conﬁrmed the diagnosis of
severe aortic stenosis (AS) and computed tomography
(CT) revealed the penetrating aortic ulcer (PAU) in the
aortic arch.
INVESTIGATIONS
Echocardiography revealed the presence of a severe
AS (aortic valve area of 0.82 cm2, peak velocity of
4.05 m/s, and mean pressure gradient of 32 mm Hg),
mild aortic regurgitation, and mild mitral regurgita-
tion; the ejection fraction was normal. Cardiac func-
tion was classiﬁed as NYHA functional class II.
CT aortogram was done to evaluate the anatomy of
the aortic valve and aortic arch. The aortic annulus
perimeter was 78.9 mm, transcatheter aortic valve
with mild calciﬁcation mainly in the noncoronary
cusp, extending to 14.5 mm height above the annulus,
with calciﬁcation in the sinotubular junction and
ascending aorta. The rest of the details are mentioned
in Figure 1. PAU was revealed in the aortic arch
(Figure 2). Severe calciﬁcation and extremely narrow
femoral access (right iliac artery: 4.7 mm, left iliac
artery: 3.8 mm) (Figure 3).

After a heart team discussion, he was planned for a
1-stop TEVAR þ TAVR procedure by cardiologists and
vascular surgeons. The procedure was performed
under
general
anesthesia
and
transesophageal
echocardiography.
We have chosen to start with TEVAR and then
proceed with the TAVR plan. The speciﬁc process is

OUTCOME AND FOLLOW-UP
The postoperative recovery of the patient was posi-
tive. There were no symptoms of heart failure or
angina. No delayed atrioventricular block. The valve
function was good.
DISCUSSION
Severe AS with concomitant PAU presents unique
therapeutic challenges, particularly in older patients
with complex vascular anatomy. Although TAVR and
TEVAR have emerged as standard interventions for
AS and aortic pathologies, respectively,1,2 their com-
bined application necessitates meticulous procedural
sequencing.
A 1-stop TEVAR þ TAVR procedure may offer sig-
niﬁcant beneﬁts in patients with severe AS and PAU.3
In this case, because of the narrow femoral artery
approach and the presence of aortic aneurysm, we
decided to ﬁrst dilate the narrowed femoral artery
approach
before
treating
the
aortic
aneurysm.
Finally, TAVR is safer through processed entry routes.
But when performing TEVAR ﬁrst, 3 primary con-
cerns emerge: 1) displacement risk of the endovas-
cular
stent
graft
by
TAVR
delivery
systems
(particularly in type III aortic arch anatomy) requires
meticulous evaluation because the TAVR delivery
system may exert radial forces displacing the thoracic
stent graft, particularly in cases with tortuous anat-
omy; 2) the deployed stent graft creates additional
friction
resistance,
increasing
technical
difﬁculty
during subsequent device navigation; and 3) there is
also a risk of obstruction or damage to the valve or
stent when the delivery system is inserted through
the aortic stent during TAVR. Conversely, performing
TAVR before TEVAR introduces risks of aortic injury
during arch traversal, especially in patients with
severe calciﬁcation.
Our case ultimately proves that this method is safe,
effective,
and
feasible.This
case
underscores
the
complexity and challenges in managing patients with
severe
AS
and
concurrent
aortic
pathology,

CONCLUSIONS

concurrent aortic arch aneurysms, even in those
with extremely narrow vascular access. Close multi-
disciplinary collaboration is crucial for successful
outcomes in such complex cases.
