ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND Septic coronary embolism is a rare but serious complication of infective endocarditis, particularly after 
valve-in-valve transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR). Mechanical complications such as pseudoaneurysm or 
myocardial rupture are exceedingly uncommon.
CASE SUMMARY A 78-year-old man with prior surgical aortic bioprosthesis and valve-in-valve TAVR presented with 
asthenia and electrocardiographic signs of inferior ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction. Despite normal coro-
naries on angiography, blood cultures revealed Enterococcus faecalis, and transesophageal echocardiography confirmed 
aortic valve vegetation. Positron emission tomography–computed tomography showed 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose uptake 
in the spleen and left ventricular (LV) wall. On day 15, he developed hemothorax and anemia; transthoracic echocar-
diography revealed an LV free wall pseudoaneurysm. Emergent surgery confirmed LV rupture and communication with 
the pleural space, repaired via Teflon “sandwich” patch.
DISCUSSION This case illustrates an unusual presentation of septic coronary embolism leading to myocardial rupture 
without tamponade, highlighting the diagnostic value of multimodal imaging.
TAKE-HOME MESSAGE Clinicians should remain vigilant for structural complications of post-TAVR infective 
endocarditis, where early diagnosis and surgical management are lifesaving. (JACC Case Rep. 2025;30:105526)

DIFFERENTIAL DIAGNOSIS
The differential diagnoses included acute coronary 
syndrome, prosthetic valve endocarditis, cardiogenic 
shock, sepsis, cerebral embolism, diabetic ketoaci-
dosis, pleural effusion, neoplasia, and anemia.
INVESTIGATIONS
The patient was admitted to the cardiology unit. 
Initial laboratory tests revealed high-sensitivity 
troponin I of 1,313 ng/L and B-type natriuretic pep-
tide of 7,228 pg/mL. Medical treatment of STEMI was 
started (unfractionated heparin, clopidogrel, aspirin, 
beta-blockers). Chest radiograph showed no pleural
effusion. Transthoracic echocardiography showed 
moderate stenosis of the TAVR bioprosthesis and 
reduced left ventricular (LV) ejection fraction of 45%. 
Coronary angiography revealed no obstructive coro-
nary artery disease (Figure 2).
Owing to persistent fever and inflammatory 
markers (C-reactive protein: 267 mg/L, white blood 
cell count: 18.7 × 109/L), blood cultures were ob-
tained, 
yielding 
Enterococcus 
faecalis. 
Trans-
esophageal echocardiography identified an 8-mm 
aortic valve vegetation (Figure 3). Positron emission 
tomography–computed tomography demonstrated 
increased 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) uptake in 
the spleen and LV wall; cerebral emboli were not 
found (Figure 4).

Given the absence of criteria for surgically treating 
the endocarditis (aortic vegetation <10 mm, stable 
hemodynamics), the endocarditis team decided to 
treat the patient with medical therapy alone 
(amoxicillin + ceftriaxone). 1
On day 15 of hospitalization, the patient developed 
acute left chest pain and severe anemia (hemoglobin: 
7 g/dL vs 11.7 g/dL at admission). Chest radiograph 
showed a large pleural effusion (Figure 5). Emergent 
chest tube placement drained hematic fluid, and 
repeat transthoracic echocardiography revealed a 
pseudoaneurysm of the LV free wall communicating 
with the pleural space (Figure 6, Video 1).
MANAGEMENT
Given the acute deterioration and imaging consistent 
with LV free wall pseudoaneurysm, the case was 
reviewed by a multidisciplinary heart team. With a 
EuroSCORE II of 20%, the patient was taken for 
emergent surgery. The surgical procedure consisted 
of redo sternotomy, cardiopulmonary bypass (aortic 
and right atrium cannulation), aortic vent (only for 
de-airing purposes), and beating heart repair. Intra-
operatively, a 3-cm LV (posterolateral) free wall 
rupture with communication to the pleural space was 
confirmed (Figure 7).
The pseudoaneurysm was incised, and the clots 
were removed. Surgical repair was performed using a 
double-layered 
Teflon 
patch 
(modified 
linear 
closure). The incision was closed using 2-0 Prolene 
mattress sutures buttressed with felt strips. The

OUTCOME AND FOLLOW-UP
The patient’s recovery was favorable in the immedi-
ate postoperative course. He remained in the inten-
sive care unit for 8 days. His LV ejection fraction was 
stable to 40%, and he completed a 6-week course of 
intravenous antibiotics. He was discharged in stable 
condition after 2 months of hospitalization. During 
the 3-month follow-up, the patient subsequently
underwent echocardiography-guided percutaneous 
drainage of the splenic abscess.
DISCUSSION
Septic coronary embolism is a rare but severe 
complication of infective endocarditis, occurring in 
2% to 3% of cases. 1 Mechanical complications such as 
myocardial rupture or pseudoaneurysm are rarer 
still, particularly in prosthetic valve endocarditis 
after ViV-TAVR. 3,4
This case illustrates the diagnostic challenge and 
high morbidity associated with valve-in-valve

It is important to highlight the unique aspects of 
this clinical case. The diagnosis of coronary embo-
lism was only established retrospectively, after the 
pseudoaneurysm had progressed and ruptured. A 
comparison between the coronary angiography per-
formed at admission and the one obtained in 2022 
when the patient underwent ViV-TAVR revealed a 
new filling defect at the bifurcation of the first obtuse 
marginal branch, consistent with a septic embolus 
(Figure 2, Video 2).
What is particularly striking is that an embolic 
event involving such a small coronary branch resul-
ted in such a severe complication. Cardiac pseudoa-
neurysms are typically reported in association with 
occlusion of large-caliber coronary arteries such as 
the left anterior descending or right coronary artery. 3 
Another unusual feature of this case is that the 
myocardial rupture did not present as pericardial 
tamponade but rather as a massive left-sided hemo-
thorax, owing to fistulization of the LV into the 
pericardial and pleural spaces. This atypical presen-
tation may be explained by prior sternotomy-related 
adhesions and the septic nature of the pseudoa-
neurysm. Positron emission tomography–computed 
tomography revealed increased FDG uptake in the 
LV free wall, which was initially thought to be 
nonspecific but retrospectively supported the diag-
nosis of septic coronary embolism.
Furthermore, this case raises the question of 
whether the “excess” prosthetic material in ViV-
TAVR procedures may contribute to infection risk. 
The TAVR stent frame lies in close proximity to the 
coronary ostia, and once infected, it may potentially 
promote septic embolization. However, this mecha-
nism has not yet been validated in clinical studies 
and remains speculative, based solely on this isolated 
observation.
CONCLUSIONS
Clinicians should maintain vigilance for coronary and 
structural complications in infective endocarditis 
after ViV-TAVR. Early recognition and prompt sur-
gical intervention, guided by a multidisciplinary 
heart team, are critical in managing these life-
threatening events.
