HOW WE DID IT
Aortic Valve Stenosis and Insufficiency 
Combined With Aortic Coarctation
Tao Hu, MM, Weiling Tu, MM, Linfeng Li, MD
ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND The coexistence of aortic valve stenosis and regurgitation with native coarctation is a rare and complex 
condition in adults, especially those with significant surgical risks, complicating management.
CASE SUMMARY A 60-year-old male with poorly controlled hypertension, chronic kidney disease, and atrial fibril-
lation presented with progressive dyspnea and chest tightness. Echocardiography revealed a bicuspid aortic valve with 
moderate stenosis and regurgitation, reduced ejection fraction, and left ventricular dilation. Computed tomography 
showed near-occlusive coarctation of the descending aorta. Given high surgical risk, conventional surgery was deemed 
unfeasible.
DISCUSSION A single-session transcatheter “one-stop” approach, combining balloon angioplasty, aortic valve 
replacement, and stent implantation, was performed, resulting in immediate hemodynamic improvement.
TAKE-HOME MESSAGE A unified, single-session transcatheter strategy offers a safe and effective alternative for 
high-risk patients with aortic valve disease and coarctation. (JACC Case Rep. 2026;31:106415) © 2026 The Authors.

INVESTIGATIONS
TTE demonstrated a bicuspid aortic valve with 
degenerative changes, moderate stenosis (peak ve-
locity 3.7 m/s), moderate-to-severe regurgitation 
(Figure 1) (regurgitant area 6.9 cm 2 ), reduced left 
ventricular ejection fraction (43%), left ventricular 
dilatation (63 mm), and concentric hypertrophy 
(16 mm, LVPWd 14 mm) (Figure 2). Small pericardial 
effusion was also present.
CTA of the thoracoabdominal aorta and coronary 
arteries provided critical anatomical details (Figures 3 
and 4): a severe, nearly occlusive coarctation just 
distal to the left subclavian artery origin, with 
extensive collateral circulation and localized post-
stenotic dilatation (Figures 5 and 6). Coronary arteries 
showed mild atherosclerosis without significant 
obstruction.
MANAGEMENT
The patient was diagnosed with severe aortic valve 
disease (combined stenosis and regurgitation), aortic 
coarctation, cardiomegaly, persistent atrial fibrilla-
tion, and NYHA functional class III heart failure. With 
an elevated Society of Thoracic Surgeons STS risk 
score of 9.13%, he was considered a prohibitive 
candidate for traditional surgical repair (aortic valve 
replacement plus coarctation correction). A multi-
disciplinary 
heart 
team 
recommended 
a 
transcatheter-based “one-stop” strategy to address 
both pathologies sequentially in a single session.
The procedure was performed 1 week after 
admission under general anesthesia with trans-
esophageal echocardiographic (TEE) guidance.
Step 1: Coarctation balloon angioplasty. After 
placement of a temporary pacemaker via the left 
femoral vein, the left radial and right femoral arteries 
were accessed. A JR4 catheter was advanced from the 
radial artery, and a 0.035-inch guidewire was care-
fully maneuvered across the severely narrowed 
coarctation segment. The wire was snared from the 
right femoral artery to establish a stable through-
and-through rail.
A 8 × 120-mm peripheral balloon was then used to 
dilate the coarctation at 12 atm (Figure 7), resulting in 
significant improvement in the lumen diameter 
without immediate complications like dissection 
(Figure 8).
Step 2: Transcatheter aortic valve replacement 
(TAVR). Following successful angioplasty, attention 
was turned to the aortic valve. The right femoral
access was upsized to a 22-F sheath. After crossing 
the stenotic valve with an AL1 catheter and a stiff 
wire, balloon valvuloplasty was performed with an 
18 × 40-mm balloon. A 27-mm self-expanding 
VitaFlow valve (MicroPort Shanghai) was then 
deployed accurately in the aortic annulus (Figure 9) 
under rapid pacing and angiographic guidance. Post 
deployment, a minor paravalvular leak was noted, 
which was successfully sealed with a 22 × 40-mm 
balloon post dilation. Final TEE showed excellent 
valve function with a peak gradient of 1.3 m/s and 
only trace paravalvular leak.

Step 3: Cheatham-Platinum (CP) stent implanta-
tion. With the aortic valve successfully treated, the 
coarctation site was secured. A 14-F delivery sheath 
was advanced over the existing stiff wire, and a 
covered CP stent was deployed precisely at the pre-
viously dilated coarctation segment, just distal to the
left subclavian artery (Figure 10). Final angiography 
confirmed optimal stent expansion and resolution of 
the aortic narrowing without endoleak (Figure 11). 
The entire procedure was completed in approxi-
mately 2 hours without any acute complications. The 
patient was transferred to the Cardiac Care Unit in 
stable condition.
OUTCOME AND FOLLOW-UP
The patient’s hemodynamic status improved imme-
diately. Blood pressure normalized without any 
antihypertensive medications. The temporary pace-
maker was removed on postoperative day 3. He was 
discharged 1 week later in stable condition. At 
1-month follow-up, he reported complete resolution 
of symptoms. TTE confirmed a well-functioning 
prosthetic valve with a peak gradient of 1.9 m/s and 
mild paravalvular leak.
Blood pressure remained controlled.
DISCUSSION
This case demonstrates the feasibility and efficacy of 
a combined transcatheter approach for high-risk pa-
tients with complex aortic valve disease and 
congenital CoA. The “one-stop” strategy offers 
several advantages: it reduces risks and recovery 
time from multiple surgeries, improves hemody-
namics by alleviating left ventricular outflow tract 
obstruction and increased systemic afterload and 
shortens hospital stays, ultimately reducing costs. 5 
CoA is frequently associated with bicuspid aortic 
valve (50% to 85% prevalence), 6 which significantly 
influences valve selection and procedural planning. 
The bicuspid anatomy combined with coarctation 
increases complexity in device delivery and valve 
sizing, requiring careful assessment to minimize 
paravalvular leak risk.
Success depended on thorough preoperative 
planning with multimodality imaging.
CTA identified the CoA location, severity, and 
aortic wall characteristics, guiding our strategy. 
Intraoperative TEE monitoring allowed real-time 
hemodynamic assessment.
The sequence—balloon angioplasty first, followed 
by TAVR, then stent implantation—minimized device 
interaction risks and enabled staged evaluation of 
aortic response.
Balloon catheter size was carefully selected to 
relieve obstruction without excessive dilation, with 
intraoperative 
adjustments based on imaging 
feedback.
Valve selection required careful consideration. 
While balloon-expandable valves offer advantages

including lower crossing profiles (12- to 14-F vs 14- to 
16-F) and steerable delivery systems facilitating 
passage through stenosis, we selected a self-
expanding valve for: 1) superior adaptability to 
altered aortic geometry post angioplasty; 2) reduced 
radial force minimizing stress on weakened aortic 
wall; and 3) repositioning capability. However, 
balloon-expandable valves with steerable catheters 
may be preferred in cases with severe angulation or 
small 
coarctation 
sites 
requiring 
precise

Blood pressure control was more stringent than in 
typical TAVR due to hemodynamic instability risk 
from CoA. Vasopressors maintained cardiac perfu-
sion while ensuring stable pressure after balloon 
dilation and minimizing fluctuations during valve 
implantation.
Aortic dissection was a critical concern. CoA 
frequently associates with intrinsic aortopathy 
(medial 
degeneration, 
poststenotic 
dilatation), 
increasing injury susceptibility during balloon dila-
tion. 7 Preoperative CTA identified wall thickness and 
calcification patterns, informing balloon sizing to 
avoid 
excessive 
stress. 
Emergency 
protocols 
included backup covered stents and extracorporeal 
circulation equipment. 8
The covered CP stent placement provided addi-
tional protection against delayed complications.
This approach employs staged balloon angioplasty 
before stent deployment, allowing aortic response 
assessment before permanent device commitment. 
Despite potential challenges including contrast-
induced nephropathy or vascular complications, 
careful patient selection, comprehensive planning, 
and fluoroscopic guidance ensured successful 
execution.
In conclusion, this single-session transcatheter 
approach represents a safe and effective strategy 
for high-risk patients with combined aortic valve 
disease and coarctation. Through meticulous plan-
ning, rational device selection, and comprehensive 
risk mitigation, we achieved excellent outcomes 
while minimizing procedural risks and hospital 
stay.
CONCLUSIONS
For high-risk patients with combined aortic valve 
and coarctation pathology, a single-session trans-
catheter approach using balloon angioplasty, TAVR, 
and CP stent implantation is a safe, effective, and 
efficient strategy. 7,9 It offers excellent immediate-
and short-term outcomes, highlighting its potential 
as a valuable option for the heart team. Further
experience and long-term follow-up are needed to 
validate this approach. 6,7,9,10
