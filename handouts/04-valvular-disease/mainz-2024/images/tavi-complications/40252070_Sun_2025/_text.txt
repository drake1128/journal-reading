VALVULAR HEART DISEASE
CLINICAL CASE
Valve Dragging Technique in Valve
Migration After Self-Expanding
Transcatheter Aortic Valve Replacement
Yinghao Sun, MD, Jie Li, MD, Yuan Liu, MD, Jianfang Luo, MD, PHD
ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND Transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) may be considered for patients with symptomatic
severe aortic regurgitation at high surgical risk.
CASE SUMMARY This article presents the case of a 74-year-old man who presented with dyspnea and was diagnosed
with severe aortic regurgitation. TAVR with a self-expanding valve was performed but complicated with valve migration
toward the left ventricle, resulting in severe paravalvular regurgitation. Ultimately, the valve dragging technique was
used to reposition the initial valve and a second valve was implanted, achieving satisfactory ﬁnal outcomes.
DISCUSSION This technique provides a novel strategy for valve migration after TAVR, avoiding surgical reintervention.
TAKE-HOME MESSAGE The valve dragging technique is a remedial measure for severe paravalvular regurgitation
secondary to valve migration after self-expanding TAVR by dragging with a second valve. (JACC Case Rep.

PAST MEDICAL HISTORY
The patient had hyperlipidemia and hyper-
tension. The Society of Thoracic Surgeons 30-
Day Predicted Risk of Mortality (STS-PROM)
score was calculated to be 1.49%. After dis-
cussion by the heart team, he was judged high risk for
surgery because of frailty caused by osteoarthritis of
both
knees
and
sequelae
of
cerebral
infarction.
Transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) was
performed considering high surgical risk and patient
preference. Computed tomography (CT) showed pe-
ripheral artery suitable for TAVR. The CT measure-
ment is shown in Figure 1. The perimeter-derived
diameter of the aortic annulus was 24.1 mm (left
ventricular outﬂow tract 24.0 mm, sinotubular junc-
tion 42.1 mm, ascending aorta 41.0 mm), the aortic
root angle was large (78), and leaﬂet calciﬁcation was
not present. No signiﬁcant stenosis was detected on
coronary CT angiography.
DEVICE AND PROCEDURE
The prosthesis used is the new-generation retrievable
self-expanding VitaFlow (Shanghai MicroPort), which
consists of a cylindrical nitinol frame and a tri-leaﬂet
bovine pericardial valve. It has an inner and outer
skirt to prevent paravalvular regurgitation. It can be
released using an electric handle. The prosthesis is
manufactured in 4 different sizes (21, 24, 27, and
30 mm) (Figure 2). The safety and efﬁcacy of trans-
femoral TAVR using VitaFlow in high-risk severe
aortic regurgitation has been reported previously.1
First, a 6-F pigtail catheter was passed across the
aortic valve and was then exchanged with the Lun-
derquist wire to establish a TAVR pathway through
the right femoral artery. Another 6-F pigtail catheter
was inserted through left femoral artery to the non-
coronary sinus for angiography. A prosthesis Vita-
Flow 27 mm (with a 29-mm inﬂow end offering 20%
oversizing rate) was ﬁnally released at an ideal posi-
tion after the fourth attempt due to challenging
anatomy, but valve migration occurred just a few
seconds
after
the
deployment,
and
angiography
showed
severe
paravalvular
aortic
regurgitation
(Figure 3, Video 1). Hemodynamics deteriorated and
transesophageal echocardiography showed compres-
sion of mitral chordae, causing acute mitral regurgi-
tation. We decided to apply the valve dragging
technique as a bailout procedure. A second valve of
the same size was positioned a little higher than the
ﬁrst one (Figure 4A) and half released under rapid
pacing
(Figure
4B).
The
operator
cautiously
and
slowly dragged back the delivery system until the ﬁrst
valve moved upward to the original ideal position
(Figure 4C, Video 2), then ﬁnally released the second
valve. The ﬁnal angiography showed stable position
of both prostheses and no signiﬁcant paravalvular
regurgitation (Figure 4D, Video 3). The patient was
discharged without other complications. The echo at

DISCUSSION
For patients with symptomatic severe aortic regurgi-
tation, guidelines recommend surgery as the ﬁrst-line
approach; however, TAVR may be considered for
those at high surgical risk.2 Studies indicate that the
success rate of treating pure aortic regurgitation with
new-generation self-expanding valves has signiﬁ-
cantly
improved.3
In
this
case,
the
patient
was
deemed high risk because of frailty and ultimately
underwent TAVR after multidisciplinary discussion.
At present, a speciﬁc transfemoral TAVR device for
aortic regurgitation was not available in our hospital.

When valve migration occurred after TAVR, we
aimed to resolve the issue via interventional methods
before resorting to open heart surgery. A common
approach is direct implantation of a second valve.
However, the ﬁrst valve had interfered with the
anterior mitral leaﬂet motion observed by ultrasound

duction block because of implant depth. It was

Although the “Double Snare” technique has been re-
ported,4 its complexity, the risk of uneven stent
force, and potential of valve embolization toward the
ascending aorta prompted us to employ the second
valve to perform the “valve dragging technique.” This
allowed even upward repositioning of the ﬁrst valve.

The valve dragging technique is primarily appli-
cable to pure aortic regurgitation. This technique
may not work for all self-expanding valves. The
VitaFlow valve has a straight frame, which is easier
to drag. It also has an incurved crown, which is less
likely to cause aortic dissection. This technique also
may not be applicable to all anatomies. A dilated
ascending aorta may be a favorable feature for this
technique, as there is enough room for dragging and
the risk of aortic dissection will decrease. It is un-
suitable for cases with severe calciﬁed aortic steno-
sis,
where
dragging
of
the
ﬁrst
valve
is
often
unfeasible. ALIGN-AR (A Study to Assess Safety and
Effectiveness of the JenaValve Trilogy Heart Valve
System in the Treatment of High Surgical Risk Pa-
tients With Symptomatic, Severe Aortic Regurgita-
tion) demonstrated a 95% success rate with the
JenaValve,
a
novel
dedicated
device
for
aortic
regurgitation.5 As dedicated valves become more
widely adopted, the use of self-expanding valves for
aortic regurgitation along with the incidence of valve
migration will likely decline. This technique should
be reserved for emergency scenarios due to inherent
uncertainties, because excessive dragging force may
lead to ascending aorta embolization of the ﬁrst
valve, even aortic dissection, and subsequent sur-
gery. The efﬁcacy and safety of this technique merit
further investigation.
CONCLUSIONS
The valve dragging technique is a remedial measure
used to address severe paravalvular regurgitation
secondary to the ﬁrst valve migration toward the left
ventricle after self-expanding TAVR by dragging with
a second valve. It is primarily applicable to patients
with aortic regurgitation without severe calciﬁc ste-
nosis. The safety and efﬁcacy of this technique still
require further investigation.
