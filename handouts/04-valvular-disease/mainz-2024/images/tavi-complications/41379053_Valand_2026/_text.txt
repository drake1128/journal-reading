CLINICAL CASE
Transcatheter Repair of Aortopulmonary 
Fistula With VSD Occluder After 
Surgical Aortic Valve Replacement for 
Aortic Valve Endocarditis
Hardik A. Valand, MD, a,b Triston Smith, MD, c David Lasorda, DO b
ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND Infective endocarditis can lead to the rare and potentially fatal aortopulmonary fistula.
CASE SUMMARY We report a 52-year-old man with methicillin-sensitive Staphylococcus aureus bacteremia and aortic 
valve endocarditis complicated by a left middle cerebral artery stroke. After emergent aortic sinus debridement and 
valve replacement, imaging revealed an aortopulmonary fistula. Left-to-right shunting was confirmed. As traditional 
surgical repair was unsuitable, a ventricular septal defect occluder was deployed via a transcatheter approach. 
Follow-ups confirmed a well-seated device, mild pulmonic regurgitation, and no symptoms.
DISCUSSION Effectively managing aortopulmonary fistulas in patients with infective endocarditis is a multidisciplinary 
process combining advanced imaging techniques, hemodynamic assessment, and an innovative percutaneous approach.

CASE SUMMARY
A previously healthy 52-year-old man was transferred 
to a tertiary facility for neurological evaluation after 
developing 
methicillin-sensitive 
Staphylococcus 
aureus bacteremia and aortic valve endocarditis 
complicated by a left middle cerebral artery stroke. 
This was deemed not amenable to mechanical
thrombectomy. During the course of management, 
brain magnetic resonance imaging revealed a hypo-
dense lesion in the M2 segment. Transesophageal 
echocardiography (TEE) demonstrated a bicuspid 
aortic valve, a preserved ejection fraction, severe 
aortic insufficiency (4+), and an aortic root abscess. 
Consequently, the patient underwent emergent aortic 
sinus debridement and reconstruction with a bovine 
patch (XenoSure, LeMaitre Vascular), along with 
aortic valve replacement using a 23-mm On-X medical 
bileaflet mechanical heart valve (Artivion). Post-
operative TEE revealed no complications.
Cardiac computed tomography angiography per-
formed 1 month postoperatively was part of our 
institutional surveillance strategy for high-risk 
infective endocarditis patients, aimed for early 
detection of structural complications. The scan 
demonstrated a 6-mm tract between the pulmonary 
annulus and a contrast outpouching at the aortic 
root, consistent with an aortopulmonary fistula 
(Figure 1). After a comprehensive evaluation, the 
patient was deemed a poor surgical candidate and 
was referred to the interventional cardiology team 
for percutaneous closure. The fistula and its prox-
imity to the left main coronary artery was carefully 
assessed.

A right heart catheterization revealed the following 
pressures: right atrial mean pressure 6 mm Hg, right 
ventricular pressure 29/1/9 mm Hg, pulmonary artery 
(PA) pressure 29/14/20 mm Hg, mean pulmonary 
capillary wedge pressure 11 mm Hg, and pulmonary

Oxymetric and flow analysis confirmed significant 
left-to-right shunting (pulmonary-to-systemic flow 
ratio of 1.65:1) and an oxygen step-up in the PA. Aortic 
root angiography clearly delineated the fistula track, 
with brisk flow from the right sinus of Valsalva to the 
PA (Figure 2A, Video 1A), corroborating the findings on 
TEE (Figure 3A, Video 2A).
A 6-F pigtail catheter was advanced through a 6-F 
sheath in the right femoral vein and positioned in the 
PA. Pulmonary angiography was then performed to 
visualize the proximity of the fistula. A 6-F XB 3.5 
guiding catheter (Cordis) was advanced into the 
aortic root. A 0.035-inch Glidewire (Terumo) was 
advanced across the fistula into the PA. The fistula 
diameter was measured at 12 mm by TEE. A 14-mm 
Amplatzer atrial septal defect occluder was initially 
used, but owing to concerns about its interaction 
with the pulmonic valve and subsequent moderate to 
severe pulmonic insufficiency observed on TEE, the
occluder was removed. The 0.035-inch guidewire was 
exchanged for a 0.018-inch Steelcore wire (Abbott). 
A 9-F 45 ◦ Amplatzer TorqVue guide catheter (Abbott) 
facilitated the advancement of a 14-mm Amplatzer 
VSD device (Abbott) via the femoral artery into the 
fistula into the PA. The device was successfully 
deployed, as confirmed by stable postdeployment tug 
test. The angiogram revealed no coronary obstruc-
tion. Mild pulmonic insufficiency was observed on 
TEE (Figure 3B, Video 2B). The device was then 
released (Figure 2B, Video 1B). 
At the 1-month follow-up, the patient reported no 
symptoms, with TTE showing an ejection fraction 
of 55% to 60%, mild pulmonic regurgitation, and a 
well-seated device without any leak. A 12-month 
follow-up visit also showed no physiologic or 
anatomic changes. These evaluations were per-
formed in line with our institutional protocol, which 
involves scheduled transthoracic echocardiography 
at 1, 6, and 12 months postprocedure and annually 
thereafter to assess device position, residual shunt, 
and valve function. In addition, periodic hemolysis 
panels, including lactate dehydrogenase, hapto-
globin, and indirect bilirubin, along with complete 
blood counts, are obtained at each visit to monitor 
for device-related hemolysis. All laboratory values 
remained within normal limits at the 1-, 6-, and 
12-month follow-up visits.

DISCUSSION
Infective endocarditis of the aortic valve necessi-
tates aggressive surgical intervention, particularly 
when complicated by severe aortic insufficiency 
and abscess formation. However, postoperative 
complications such as aortopulmonary fistulas pose 
significant challenges in patient management. 1,2 
This case is unique, as it represents to our 
knowledge the first reported instance of success-
fully managing an aortopulmonary fistula secondary 
to endocarditis and surgical aortic valve replace-
ment using a VSD occluder via a transcatheter 
approach.
Staphylococcus species are the most common or-
ganism associated with aortopulmonary fistula 
in patients with bacteremia. 4 In our case, the likely 
mechanism was infection-related tissue destruction 
from infective endocarditis rather than iatrogenic 
injury from bypass cannulation. Importantly, there 
was no evidence of a fistula preoperatively, as 
confirmed by intraoperative TEE and direct surgical 
inspection. Our patient’s clinical course was further 
complicated by a left middle cerebral artery stroke and 
severe aortic insufficiency, necessitating emergent 
surgical intervention, including aortic sinus debride-
ment and valve replacement.
Traditionally aortopulmonary fistulas are managed 
by open surgical repair, which was deemed unsuitable 
for this high-risk patient. A few minimally invasive or 
combined approaches, including a balloon catheter, 
endovascular repair using coils, covered stents, and 
occluder device to close aortopulmonary fistula, have 
been reported in the literature. 5-10
A VSD occluder was particularly suitable for clos-
ing our patient’s aortopulmonary fistula given its 
specific features and anatomical considerations. The 
VSD device minimizes the disk overhang with the 
fistula, thereby reducing the risk of impinging adja-
cent structures, particularly the pulmonic valve. 
Additionally, the arterial-side approach had a more 
favorable angle for deploying the VSD device. This 
method precisely placed the device, ensuring that 
the occluder was securely positioned to effectively 
seal the fistula.
These characteristics underscore the suitability of 
the VSD for managing complex aortopulmonary fis-
tulas in high-risk patients in whom traditional sur-
gery is not feasible. The success of this minimally 
invasive approach highlights its potential as a viable 
alternative, offering valuable insights into evolving 
interventional cardiology practices.

CONCLUSIONS
This case demonstrates that effectively managing 
aortopulmonary fistulas in patients with infective 
endocarditis is a multidisciplinary process combining 
advanced 
imaging 
techniques, 
hemodynamic 
assessment, 
and 
an 
innovative 
percutaneous 
approach. Further research and case experience are 
necessary to establish expert consensus for such 
interventions.
