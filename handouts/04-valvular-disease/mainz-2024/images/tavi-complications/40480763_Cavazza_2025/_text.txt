ABSTRACT
OBJECTIVES We describe the endovascular management of multiple vessel-related complications in a case of
transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) through surgical right transaxillary access.
KEY STEPS We present the case of a TAVR performed through the right transaxillary approach and detail the treatment
of an early subclavian dissection and a subacute subclavian pseudoaneurysm, successfully managed through endovas-
cular interventions.
POTENTIAL PITFALLS Signiﬁcant subclavian disease can raise the questions whether to prepare the vessel before
the procedure and whether to proceed sheathless with the valve delivery system or use a larger ﬁxed introducer.
Additionally, the decision to treat or not treat early vessel complications may lead to different outcomes.
TAKE-HOME MESSAGES The right transaxillary approach for TAVR is a feasible alternative access, although vascular
injuries can occur. A subclavian pseudoaneurysm can be addressed with percutaneous intervention. In this scenario, loss
of the ipsilateral vertebral artery can be tolerated without serious consequences. (JACC Case Rep. 2025;30:103610)

CASE SUMMARY
An 87-year-old woman was admitted with dyspnea on
minimal exertion. Her medical history included a
previous left carotid endarterectomy, paroxysmal
atrial ﬁbrillation managed with a direct oral antico-
agulant
agent,
and
moderate
to
severe
chronic
obstructive pulmonary disease. Transthoracic echo-
cardiography
showed
paradoxical
low-ﬂow
low-
gradient
aortic
stenosis
with
preserved
ejection
fraction (55%) and a reduced stroke volume index
(28 mL/m2), as well as moderate aortic regurgitation.
Coronary angiography indicated no signiﬁcant coro-
nary artery stenosis.
To assess the feasibility of TAVR, the patient
underwent computed tomography angiography (CTA)
for procedural planning. The calcium score was 1,250,
indicating a trileaﬂet valve structure with signiﬁcant
ﬁbrosis. Thoracoabdominal imaging revealed a por-
celain ascending aorta and diffuse severe calciﬁc
disease at the abdominal level, with signs of chronic
dissection. Bilateral tight stenosis of both external
iliac arteries was observed, with minimum lumen
diameters (MLDs) <3 mm (Figure 1). Evaluation of the
subclavian
and
axillary
arteries
revealed
critical
calciﬁc
stenosis
of
the
left
subclavian
artery
(MLD <2 mm) and moderate stenosis (MLD 4.6 mm of
the right subclavian artery) (Figure 2).
The case was discussed by the multidisciplinary
heart team. Given the patient’s elevated periopera-
tive risk (The Society of Thoracic Surgeons score 8%)
and overall frailty, the decision was made to proceed
with TAVR. Femoral access was unsuitable. The
transaortic approach was excluded because of the
porcelain aorta, and left subclavian access was ruled
out
because
of
the
very
small
MLD
and
heavy
calciﬁcations. Thus, right axillary surgical access was
chosen.
The annulus dimensions (perimeter, 64.1 mm)
were suitable for implantation of a self-expandable
23-mm Evolut FX bioprosthesis (Medtronic). After
surgical exposure of the artery, the valve delivery
system was advanced sheathless to reduce French
size and vessel injury. The valve was released in the
correct position, achieving optimal ﬁnal hemody-
namic
parameters.
Final
angiography
revealed
extensive dissection at the distal one-third of the
subclavian artery, originating from
the dissected
basal
plaque,
necessitating
placement
of
a
self-
expanding stent to seal the lesion (Figure 3, Video 1).
The following day, the patient exhibited aphasia
and oral commissure deviation, being unresponsive
except to painful stimuli. CTA of the intracranial
and cervical vessels revealed small, hypodense areas
in
both
cerebellar
hemispheres,
consistent
with
subacute
ischemic
lesions,
with
no
evidence
of
hemorrhage. Regarding the axillary access site, there
was tight stenosis at the surgical closure and a pseu-
doaneurysm of the subclavian artery at the proximal
edge of the stent, measuring 8 mm  8 mm. A con-
servative strategy was initially adopted in view of the
patient’s critical condition.
The next day, the patient gradually regained con-
sciousness, with complete regression of neurologic
symptoms.
Follow-up
CTA
revealed
an
enlarged
pseudoaneurysm (maximum diameters, 11 mm 
9 mm) with early signs of wall rupture near the
proximal edge of the stent and close to the right
vertebral artery (Figures 4A to 4D). After consultation
with vascular surgeons, considering the challenging
anatomical factors, we decided to treat the axillary
stenosis
and
the
pseudoaneurysm
with
covered
stents, thereby accepting the sacriﬁce of the right
vertebral artery and hoping that the contralateral
circulation would compensate for cerebral blood ﬂow.
This intervention was performed through retrograde
right surgical humeral access (the radial artery was
not palpable), resulting in successful sealing of the
pseudoaneurysm without complications or neuro-
logic deﬁcits (Figures 5A to 5D, Video 2). The patient
was discharged 3 days post-procedure, completely
asymptomatic and in excellent neurologic condition.

POTENTIAL PITFALLS
In evaluating procedural pitfalls, we identiﬁed some
critical crossroads in our planning. The ﬁrst concerns
vessel
preparation
before
TAVR.
Placing
a
self-
expanding stent to seal the dissected plaque of the
subclavian artery could have been a viable option;
however, this strategy was deemed risky because of
the potential for crushing the newly implanted stent
while
advancing
the
valve
delivery
system
or
introducer.
The second critical issue was the decision to
advance the valve delivery system sheathless, which
may have been a suboptimal choice. Using an 18-F
introducer, advanced at least to the origin of the ca-
rotid artery, although larger, could have better pro-
tected the subclavian vessel by reducing mechanical
stress during maneuvers for valve repositioning and
recapture.
Another consideration was the choice to treat the
iatrogenic subclavian dissection immediately after
valve deployment. The entry tear extended in a
distal-to-proximal
direction,
suggesting
potential
future
spontaneous
healing
of
the
dissection.

CONCLUSIONS
This case highlights the complexities and risks of
TAVR in high-risk patients, particularly those with
signiﬁcant vascular disease. Transaxillary access is a
recognized and viable approach; however, it does not
eliminate the risk of signiﬁcant vascular injuries, for
which an endovascular approach is often the only
feasible solution given the high surgical risk. In the
case of a pseudoaneurysm arising from the proximal
end of the subclavian artery, the sacriﬁce of the
ipsilateral vertebral artery while placing a covered
stent can sometimes be well tolerated, even in fragile
patients.
Finally, this case emphasizes the importance of a
multidisciplinary
approach
in
optimizing
patient
outcomes and suggests that careful consideration of
access routes and techniques is crucial for minimizing
procedural risks in advanced cardiac interventions.
