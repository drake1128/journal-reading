CASE REPORT
CLINICAL CASE
Rescue Percutaneous Stenting for
Cerebral Malperfusion With Acute
Aortic Dissection Following TAVR
Ryo Yamaguchi, MD,a Masanori Yamamoto, MD,a,b,c Tetsuro Shimura, MD,b Yuya Adachi, MD,a Mitsuru Sago, CEa
ABSTRACT
Malperfusion syndrome is a complication of acute aortic dissection (AAD) involving a branch vessel. We report a case of
bailout stenting for critical cerebral malperfusion in a patient with AAD after transcatheter aortic valve replacement.
Rescue percutaneous procedure for this complication may be a treatment option in patients with high surgical risk.

HISTORY OF PRESENTATION
A 79-year-old man presented with dyspnea on effort
for 1 year. On physical examination, he had a systolic
ejection murmur over the right second intercostal
space.
Moreover,
his
blood
pressure
was
129/
69 mm Hg, and his heart rate was 65 beats/min. He
received a diagnosis of severe aortic stenosis. Dys-
pnea
progressed
to
New
York
Heart
Association
functional class III; therefore, he was admitted to our
cardiology center (Toyohashi Heart Center, Toyoha-
shi, Aichi, Japan). We decided to perform trans-
catheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) because the
patient was deemed unﬁt for open heart surgery as a
result of his high surgical risk.
PAST MEDICAL HISTORY
His medical history included prostate cancer with
bone metastasis and granulocytosis, for which the
patient was taking prednisolone.

Although the patient’s condition was stable without
any symptoms, contrast-enhanced CT revealed acute
aortic dissection (AAD) with a patent false lumen (FL)
limited to the ascending aorta. The sinus of Valsalva
and proximal THV edge were the suspected locations

the aortic arch at the point of the brachiocephalic
artery (Figures 3A and 3B).
MANAGEMENT
On the basis of the CT ﬁndings, the emergency sur-
gical operation was recommended because the true
lumen was severely compressed by the FL in the
ascending aorta. However, the heart team hesitated
to perform surgical repair of the dissected aorta
because of the patient’s history of prostate cancer
with bone metastasis, long-term steroid use, and
granulocytosis. The aortic dissection was limited to
the
ascending
aorta
and
did
not
involve
the
brachiocephalic artery; thus, we chose con-
servative management. On the second day
after TAVR, the patient’s level of conscious-
ness suddenly deteriorated, and left-sided
hemiparesis developed. Repeat CT demon-
strated the progression of dissection to the
aortic arch, which caused severe stenosis of
the brachiocephalic artery with near occlu-
sion of the right common carotid artery and
severe stenosis of the left common carotid
artery (Figures 3C and 3D). The catastrophic phe-
nomenon of cerebral malperfusion following AAD
should be managed using an invasive method. We
decided to perform emergency percutaneous repair
for cerebral malperfusion because the symptoms of
stroke persisted. The dissected right common carotid
artery
was
almost
occluded,
and
advancing
the
guidewire into the true lumen through the femoral
access seemed difﬁcult. Therefore, the guidewire was
passed through the true lumen from the right radial
artery to the brachiocephalic artery. Thereafter, the
guidewire was caught in retrograde fashion by using a
gooseneck snare, which was inserted from the left
femoral artery. A bare metal stent (Epic Vascular
12 mm/42 mm, Boston Scientiﬁc) was deployed to the
brachiocephalic
artery
(Video
3).

DISCUSSION
AAD was observed on aortic angiography, although
the
patient was
asymptomatic
immediately
after
TAVR. The unusually sharp aortic silhouette on aortic
angiography is a clue to the diagnosis of AAD after
TAVR. In this case, the deﬁnitive cause of AAD during
TAVR was unclear; however, the contrast leakage into
the FL was found in the sinus of Valsalva (the
recapture site of THV) and on the proximal edge of
the THV on the basis of postprocedural CT ﬁndings. It
was difﬁcult to distinguish between these 2 entry
points of AAD. Thus, we speculated that the following
mechanisms of AAD development were possible: 1)
distal THV edge-induced aortic injury while using the
THV recapture system; or 2) proximal THV edge-
induced aortic injury. The incidence of iatrogenic
AAD
after
TAVR
ranges
from
0.2%
to
2%
(1,2).
Although there are a few case reports describing the
conservative management of AAD resulting from
TAVR (3), acute organ malperfusion caused by an
iatrogenic AAD requires timely surgical or catheter
intervention. In general, the standard treatment of
AAD
with
brain
malperfusion
is
surgical
aortic
reconstruction; however, AAD causing disturbance in
consciousness is associated with increased mortality
after surgical repair (4). Conversely, 1 report showed
the effectiveness of arch vessel stenting after surgical
aortic repair for patients with persistent brain mal-
perfusion (5). Furthermore, peripheral intervention
before central aortic repair would prevent severe or-
gan damage, which leads to the improvement of
clinical outcomes (6,7).
This case may also support the effectiveness of the
“intervention-ﬁrst” strategy as an adequate alterna-
tive treatment option for patients with high and
prohibitive surgical risk. The additional left carotid
artery stenting was performed because the dissection
was
rapidly
extended,
and
precise
neurologic
assessment was difﬁcult to perform in an intubated
patient. However, there is some debate over the de-
cision to perform stenting into the left carotid artery
on the basis of the risk-to-beneﬁt balance of invasive
therapy.

FOLLOW-UP
The patient visited our center 3 months after TAVR on
foot. Follow-up CT examination conﬁrmed complete
resolution
of
the
FL.
Echocardiography
revealed
normal THV function.
CONCLUSIONS
Rescue percutaneous stenting is a useful bailout
method for cerebral malperfusion secondary to AAD
following TAVR. This technique may be considered in
emergency situations for patients with high and
prohibitive surgical risk.
