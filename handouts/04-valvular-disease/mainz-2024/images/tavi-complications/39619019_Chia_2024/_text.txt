ABSTRACT
This clinical case series reviews the diagnosis and management of aorto–right ventricular ﬁstulas post-transcatheter
aortic valve replacement, highlighting 3 cases. We emphasize early detection, individualized treatment strategies, and the
role of multidisciplinary heart teams in achieving favorable outcomes. Comprehensive imaging and procedural planning
are crucial for successful intervention and management of this rare complication. (JACC Case Rep. 2024;29:102655)

PATIENT 3
An 80-year-old woman with severe AS who under-
went TAVR (29-mm CORE valve [Medtronic]) 4 years
prior, heart failure with preserved ejection fraction,
type 2 diabetes mellitus, and chronic kidney disease
stage 3, presented with worsening chest pain and
lower extremity edema. Elevated troponins raised
concerns for non–ST-segment elevation myocardial
infarction. Catheterization revealed no signiﬁcant
coronary stenosis but a mean aortic valve gradient of
42 mm Hg. Subsequent TTE showed severe prosthetic
valve stenosis (peak gradient: 94 mm Hg; mean
gradient: 52 mm Hg; and aortic valve area: 0.69 cm2).
The pre-TAVR CTA, including the aortic annulus,
previous TAVR stent diameters, and aortic root, is
shown in Figure 8 and Table 1. An accurate aortic
valve calcium score could not be assessed because of
the presence of an old TAVR stent frame. The patient
underwent TAV-in-TAV with a 23-mm SAPIEN 3 Ultra
valve, signiﬁcantly improving symptoms. Post-TAVR
TTE on day 1 showed a 2- to 3-mm ﬁstula from the
right coronary sinus of Valsalva to the mid-RV.

RV size and function and no pulmonary hyperten-

was

Echocardio-

the patient remains asymptomatic.
DISCUSSION
ARVFs post-TAVR represent a rare yet signiﬁcant
complication.
ARVFs
are
characterized
by
an
abnormal connection between the aorta and the RV
with continuous shunt ﬂow on color and spectral
Dopplers, leading to a wide spectrum of clinical pre-
sentations, from asymptomatic to signiﬁcant hemo-
dynamic
compromise.
Our
clinical
case
series
highlights the importance of early diagnosis, symp-
tom assessment, and a well-deﬁned management
strategy for improved outcomes. Undiagnosed or
delayed diagnosis can lead to RV volume overload
caused
by
continuous
shunting
from
the
high-
pressure
aorta
to
the
low-pressure
RV
during
diastole and systole, resulting in pulmonary hyper-
tension, RV compromise, and eventually right-sided
heart failure.3,4
The exact mechanism of ARVF formation post-
TAVR is
uncertain. It is
postulated that several

pathologic mechanisms contribute to ARVF forma-
tion, most prominently involving the transmural
translocation of focal calciﬁcations from the aortic
cusp and/or aortic root wall through the basal aortic
walls during balloon TAVR deployment. This process
results in inadvertent injury to the aortic root or
annulus, leading to an abnormal communication be-
tween the aorta and the RV.5 Anatomic factors, such
as bicuspid aortic valve, local tissue characteristics,
aortic
wall
calciﬁcation,
and
tissue
fragility,
are
thought to play a signiﬁcant role in its formation.
Operator-related issues, including catheter manipu-
lation through the aortic valve, high balloon inﬂation
pressures, and prosthetic aortic valve oversizing, may
also contribute.2 In this case series, all patients
exhibited asymmetric dense calciﬁcation of the right
coronary cusp with calciﬁcation at the base of the
aortic root wall, near the right sinus of Valsalva
(Figure 10). However, no comparative data exist on
patients with similar features who did not develop
ARVF. Further studies with larger sample sizes and
comparative data are needed to identify risk factors
for ARVF formation.
DIAGNOSIS
Diagnosing ARVFs can be challenging given their
relative rarity and clinical presentation, which can
overlap with pre-TAVR symptoms. Echocardiography
is pivotal in diagnosis, enabling visualization of the
ﬁstulous tract on color Doppler and continuous wave
Doppler, which shows continuous ﬂow in both systole
and diastole (Figure 11). This continuous ﬂow aids in
the diagnosis and helps differentiate it from adjacent
pulmonic regurgitation or aortic regurgitation with
paravalvular AR. Additionally, echocardiography al-
lows for the assessment of the hemodynamic impact
of the ﬁstula, such as RV size and function and pul-
monary artery pressure. TEE can further conﬁrm the
presence and size of the ﬁstula. Cardiac computed

MANAGEMENT
Management of ARVFs post-TAVR is challenging, and
there are no clear clinical guidelines or consensus,
particularly for patients who are asymptomatic and
have a small ﬁstula. Generally, the management
strategy is determined by the degree of symptoms,
ﬁstula size, and hemodynamic consequences. For
asymptomatic or mildly symptomatic patients (NYHA
functional classes I-II) with stable hemodynamics, a
small ﬁstula, and normal RV size and function, a
conservative approach can be considered. This in-
cludes
optimizing
preload
with
diuretics,
close
monitoring
of
symptoms,
and
echocardiographic
surveillance of the ﬁstula and RV size and function,
which
can
help
guide
the
need
for
future
intervention.

CONCLUSIONS
ARVF formation after TAVR, though rare, is a serious
complication that requires prompt diagnosis and a
patient-centered, multidisciplinary team approach to
achieve favorable outcomes. With the increasing use
of transcatheter techniques in the management of AS,
clinician awareness of this potential complication is
crucial for timely management. Continued research
and awareness are essential for enhancing our un-
derstanding and management of ARVFs after TAVR.
