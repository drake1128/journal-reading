A Novel Approach for Cerebral Embolic

in TAVR
Isaac George, MD,a,b,* Maximilian Reisinger, MD,a,* Mateusz Kachel, MD, PHD,a,c Irakli Gogorishvili, MD, PHD,d
Gvantsa Dughashvili, MD,d May Nour, MD, PHD,e Tamim M. Nazif, MDb
ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND Cerebral embolic protection devices (CEPDs) have been developed to decrease embolic brain injury
and stroke during transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR). Simplifying vascular access for a CEPD may increase
procedural efﬁciency and safety
FIRST-IN-HUMAN/EARLY REPORTS SUMMARY This is the ﬁrst published report of the use of the novel EnCompass
F2 ﬁlter, which consists of a cylindrical nitinol frame and electrospun ﬁlter. Complete cerebral embolic protection during
TAVR is possible through single femoral arterial access.
DISCUSSION Cerebral protection was feasible and safe without device-related complications, clinical stroke, or other
neurologic events. Postprocedural diffusion-weighted magnetic resonance imaging revealed only 1 very small, clinically
insigniﬁcant new brain lesion.
NOVELTY This presents the ﬁrst successful use of a CEPD in TAVR through access by a single femoral artery.

A
70-year-old man with severe aortic
stenosis presented with 3 months of
progressive
dyspnea
on
exertion
(NYHA functional class III).
PAST MEDICAL HISTORY
The patient’s medical history included hy-
pertension, hyperlipidemia, nonobstructive
coronary artery disease, and peripheral ar-
tery disease (PAD), previously treated with a
right common femoral artery (CFA) stent. His
medications included aspirin, statin, and b-
blocker.
INVESTIGATIONS AND PLAN
Preprocedural
transthoracic
echocardiography
revealed a left ventricular ejection fraction of 52%
with severe aortic stenosis (mean gradient: 62 mmHg;
aortic valve area: 0.6 cm2) and moderate left ven-
tricular hypertrophy. Computed tomography angiog-
raphy
showed
aortic
annulus
area
of
492
mm2,
calciﬁcation of the aortic valve and ascending aorta,
and the prior right CFA stent (Figure 1). After evalu-
ation by the heart team, the patient was offered
transfemoral transcatheter aortic valve replacement
(TAVR) with a 26-mm Sapien 3 transcatheter heart
valve (THV) (Edwards Lifesciences). This was planned
via left femoral artery access, given the presence of
the right CFA stent, which precluded safe right
femoral arterial access. The patient was enrolled in an
early feasibility study of the EnCompass F2 cerebral
embolic
protection
device
(CEPD),
which
was
approved by the local institutional review board. An
ipsilateral, single-side approach through the left CFA
using a double-stick technique was planned as pre-
viously described.1
EnCompass F2 CEREBRAL EMBOLIC
PROTECTION DEVICE
The EnCompass F2 is a novel CEPD featuring a self-
expanding, cylindrical nitinol frame and an electro-
spun ﬁlter with a very small pore size (30 mm average)
(Figure 2). The F2 ﬁlter is deployed proximal to the
innominate artery and achieves 360 wall apposition,
ensuring stability in the aortic arch and an adequate
seal to exclude ﬂow around the ﬁlter. An important
feature of the F2 is that it can be deployed through the
TAVR access site, where it remains tethered to a
0.015-inch retention wire that facilitates ﬁlter recap-
ture at the conclusion of the procedure. Exclusion
criteria
for
the
F2
include
severe
bilateral
PAD,
severe abdominal and/or thoracic aortic disease, and
increased aortic arch diameters (ascending >38 mm,
transverse
>27
mm)
precluding
delivery
sheath
vascular access or ﬁlter deployment.
PROCEDURAL DETAILS
Anesthesia was administered, and the patient was
prepared and draped in sterile fashion. A temporary
pacemaker lead was placed via right external jugular
venous
access.
Subsequently,
the
left
CFA
was
accessed twice in superior and inferior positions.
The superior access site, intended for the TAVR
sheath, was preclosed with 2 suture-based closure
devices (Figure 3). A pigtail catheter was placed in the
aortic root through a 6-F sheath in the inferior access
site, and a standard 14-F sheath was advanced into
the descending aorta from the superior access site
(Figure 4A). Aortography was performed to determine
the appropriate angle for TAVR deployment and
landing zone for the F2, and the pigtail catheter was
removed.
A stiff 0.035-inch guidewire was placed through
the 14-F sheath superiorly, and the F2 was advanced
on the wire to a position several centimeters proximal
to the innominate artery and deployed with a simple
pin-and-pull mechanism (Figure 4B, Video 1). After
successful placement of the F2 in the aortic arch
covering all 3 great vessels (Figure 4C), the delivery
system and 14-F sheath were removed, retaining both
the 0.015-inch ﬁlter retention wire and 0.035-inch
guidewire through the access site. The CEPD reten-
tion wire was secured to the patient externally, and a
14-F Edwards eSheath was advanced into the artery
over the stiff guidewire alone. A pigtail catheter was
readvanced from the inferior sheath, through the
center
of
the
CEPD,
and
into
the
aortic
root
(Figure
4D).
Subsequently,
a
5-F
catheter
was
advanced over the stiff guidewire, which remained
proximal to the F2 through its central lumen, and the
aortic valve was crossed in standard fashion. A stiff,
shaped guidewire was placed in the left ventricle, and

FOLLOW-UP
There were no procedural complications, and post-
operative
transthoracic
echocardiography
demon-
strated appropriate THV position and function with a
mean gradient of 10 mmHg and no signiﬁcant aortic
regurgitation.
Diffusion-weighted
magnetic
reso-
nance imaging (DW-MRI) after 24 hours demon-
strated a single new brain lesion with a volume of
only 10 mm3. During the index hospitalization and at
the 30-day follow-up visit, there was no evidence of
stroke or major adverse cardiac and cerebrovascular
events, including all-cause death, all stroke, major
vascular complications, type 2-4 bleeding, or stage
3-4 acute kidney injury.

DISCUSSION
The current report describes the ﬁrst-in-human use of
the EnCompass F2, a novel CEPD consisting of a cy-
lindrical nitinol frame and electrospun ﬁlter with a
very small pore size (30 mm average). Cerebral pro-
tection with the F2 during TAVR was feasible and
safe without device-related complications, stroke, or
other neurologic events and only 1 very small, clini-
cally insigniﬁcant new brain lesion on postprocedural
DW-MRI. This case also demonstrates the feasibility
of performing CEPD for TAVR with access by a single
femoral artery only.
Despite signiﬁcant advances in TAVR technologies
and techniques over the past decade, the incidence of
stroke within 30 days has remained at 2% to 3%.2,3
The risk of stroke is highest in the initial 24 to
72 hours after TAVR, and stroke is associated with a
signiﬁcant increase in morbidity and mortality in
TAVR recipients.4 The majority of these strokes are
ischemic in nature and are believed to be caused by
embolization of debris during catheter advancement

Recent
evidence
suggests
that
new
brain
lesion
number, size, and total volume identiﬁed by DW-MRI
after TAVR are associated with clinical ischemic
stroke, disabling stroke, and worse stroke recovery.7
CEPDs of various designs have been developed to
decrease the risk of stroke during TAVR.8,9 The only
widely available device is the Sentinel (Boston Sci-
entiﬁc), a partial intravascular ﬁlter that has been
shown to be safe and to capture embolic debris in
>99% of TAVR cases.8 However, the Sentinel has
failed to convincingly reduce stroke in randomized
trials, resulting in limited adoption (<15% of TAVR
cases in the United States).3,10 Another extensively
tested CEPD is the TriGuard 3 (Keystone Heart), an
aortic arch deﬂector covering all 3 cerebral vessels.9
Similarly to the Sentinel, the TriGuard 3 failed to
show
a
signiﬁcant
reduction
in
efﬁcacy
in
the
REFLECT II trial.9 The failure of existing CEPDs to
reduce stroke in clinical trials may be attributable to
insufﬁcient sample size, varying degrees of stroke
risk in enrolled subjects, inexperienced operators, or,
importantly, ineffective CEPD. As such, there remains
an unmet clinical need for a safe and efﬁcacious CEPD
for widespread use in TAVR.
The novel EnCompass F2 offers several potential
advantages relative to prior CEPDs. First, the self-
expanding cylindrical nitinol frame achieves 360 of
wall apposition to ensure stability in the aortic arch
and an adequate seal to exclude ﬂow around the ﬁl-
ter. In comparison, the TriGuard 3 CEPD achieves only
partial wall apposition, which leads to suboptimal
stability. In the current case, the F2 was easy to
deploy with a simple unsheathing maneuver in the
appropriate location proximal to the innominate ar-
tery, and it remained stable throughout the TAVR
without signiﬁcant interaction. Second, the electro-
spun ﬁlter has an average pore size of only 30 mm,
much
smaller
than
those
previously
available
(Sentinel: 150 mm; TriGuard 3: 115  145 mm). This may
be reﬂected in the DW-MRI results showing only a
single, very small ischemic brain lesion of 10 mm3.
For comparison, the median total new lesion volume
was 102.83 mm3 in the SENTINEL IDE trial, and a
recent analysis from the REFLECT trials demon-
strated a cutoff point of 440 to 550 mm3 to be
reasonable for predicting stroke.7,8
Whereas prior CEPDs have generally required an
additional vascular access site, the F2 can be deployed
through the TAVR access site because of the very
small 0.01500 retention wire, which remains external
to the TAVR sheath throughout the procedure. In the
current case, no bleeding or hematoma was observed
around the retention wire. With a deployment time
of <2 minutes in the current case, the F2 did not
signiﬁcantly prolong the TAVR procedure. In com-
parison, the Sentinel device required w28 minutes for
deployment in its early experience.3 The current case
also demonstrates how deployment of the F2 through
the TAVR access site can be used safely to achieve
TAVR with complete cerebral protection with access
by only a single femoral artery, which may ultimately
improve
procedural
safety.
The
use
of
a
single
femoral artery approach may have several advan-
tages. First, the risk of vascular and bleeding com-
plications may be reduced by avoiding a second
access vessel. Second, the patient described in our
case report had PAD, which previously required a
right CFA stent, which would have precluded CEPD
use. Thus, the possibility of a single femoral artery
approach may make the device suitable for a wider
range of patients, particularly those with signiﬁcant
vascular disease.
CONCLUSIONS
This is the ﬁrst-in-human report of the use of the
novel EnCompass F2 CEPD, which consists of a self-
expanding, cylindrical nitinol frame and electrospun
membrane with 30 mm average pore size, to provide
complete cerebral embolic protection during TAVR.
