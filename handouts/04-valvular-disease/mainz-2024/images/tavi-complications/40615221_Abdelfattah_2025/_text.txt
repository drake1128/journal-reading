ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND Treatment and accurate assessment of aortic valve stenosis (AS) with concomitant obstructive hy-
pertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) prove challenging.
CASE SUMMARY We present a case series of 6 patients with obstructive HCM and severe AS who underwent alcohol
septal ablation (ASA) and follow-up reassessment of AS severity. AS severity was downgraded in 3 of 6 cases. One patient
had residual obstruction necessitating mavacamten before transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR). TAVR was
performed in 2 of 6 cases.
DISCUSSION Doppler assessment of AS is technically challenging in HCM. A staged intervention with ASA and/or
mavacamten before TAVR requires thoughtful consideration. The literature supports the use of ASA in a staged fashion
before TAVR. Limited data are published on the use of mavacamten before TAVR.
TAKE-HOME MESSAGES ASA and mavacamten are effective means to relieve obstruction in patients with concomi-
tant severe AS. The beneﬁt is 2-fold: 1) this approach provides more accurate Doppler assessment of AS severity; and 2) it
reduces the likelihood of complications during TAVR. (JACC Case Rep. 2025;30:103779) © 2025 Published by Elsevier on

myopathy (oHCM) and concomitant AS,
enabling more accurate Doppler assessment

suitable for ASA, allowing safe TAVR in oHCM
with concomitant severe AS.

and reduce procedural risk in high-surgical-
risk patients with dual pathology of oHCM
and severe AS.

DISCUSSION
In this case series, we demonstrated the safety and
efﬁcacy
of
ASA
and
mavacamten
in
improving
symptoms
and
described
the
hemodynamic
and
clinical
short-term
and
midterm
outcomes
in
concomitant oHCM and severe AS. We also presented
the second case to our knowledge in which mava-
camten was used after ASA to reduce LVOTO to
perform TAVR safely.7
ASA has repeatedly been shown to be an effective
treatment in alleviating obstruction in high–surgical
risk
patients
whose
conditions
are
refractory
to
medical treatment. ASA resulting in improved LVOTO
also
allows
for
a
more
accurate
hemodynamic
assessment of AS. The use of a noninvasive modality
such
as
TTE
may
be
unreliable
in
accurately
measuring the extent of hemodynamic impact caused
by
obstructions
at
different
levels by
using
the
simpliﬁed Bernoulli equation. More importantly, the
coexistence of serial obstructions could obscure the
actual contribution of either obstruction to the pre-
sented symptoms. We presented several Doppler

In nonsurgical candidates, a staged ASA and TAVR
approach remains a reasonable alternative and should
be considered in selected patients. The challenge lies
in determining which obstruction to intervene in
ﬁrst. Several reports have documented the exacer-
bation of LVOTO post TAVR leading to suicide left
ventricle, necessitating a rescue ASA.3,4,8 Patients
with
oHCM
are
at
risk
of
suicide
left
ventricle
following AVR through several mechanisms. TAVR
leads to an immediate reduction in afterload and
increased LVOT ﬂow and a subsequent Venturi effect,
pulling the mitral valve leaﬂet anteriorly and hence
worsening
already
existing
LVOTO.
Moreover,
increased
contractility
of
the
left
ventricle
and
decreased ﬁlling pressures in patients with a hyper-
trophied small left ventricle lead to obliteration of the
LV cavity and result in subsequent hemodynamic
instability.9 Such outcomes can be devastating unless
acute interventions are pursued, including aggressive
preload resuscitation and emergency ASA to relieve
obstruction.
Our approach focused mainly on initially relieving
the LVOTO with ASA and/or CMI therapy, with in-
terval re-evaluation of symptoms and AV Doppler
assessment (Central Illustration). Most of our cohort
presented with good results post-ASA. Four of the 6
patients had a down-grading of AS severity on post-
ASA TTE. One patient continued to have obstruc-
tion,
and
another
patient
developed
recurrent
obstruction following ASA. Addressing the LVOTO
before valve intervention provided the opportunity to
assess LV hemodynamics accurately with TTE and
reclassify the severity of AS. For the 1 patient with
recurrent obstruction in follow-up, coronary anatomy
was thought to be unfavorable for repeat ASA, and the
decision was made to initiate CMI therapy. This pa-
tient was successfully treated with mavacamten,
which reduced the LVOT gradient, thereby permitting
accurate assessment of the AV mean gradient. In this
patient, AS remained moderate to severe, and the
patient went on to TAVR without intraprocedural and
or postprocedural complications.
A previous study by El-Sabawi et al5 demonstrated
promising similar short-term results in a small case
series by using ASA before TAVR. However, despite
ASA’s efﬁcacy and safety in relieving LVOTO, in some
instances, ASA may not be feasible as a result of
septal perforator anatomical distribution. This may
explain why up to 20% of patients undergoing ASA
may not experience satisfactory relief of symptoms
despite short-term success in alleviating the dynamic
LVOTO.10
An alternative and novel approach to this chal-
lenging scenario was published in a recent case report
that emphasized the imaging challenges of accurate
AV assessment with ongoing LVOTO.6 This case had
evidence of LVOTO in addition to elevated mean AV
gradients. ASA was performed without improvement
in LVOT and AV gradients. Therefore, mavacamten
was initiated and was successful in reducing the
LVOT gradient. On follow-up imaging, there was no
further evidence of AS. This issue was likely second-
ary to the technical difﬁculties of accurately assessing
the AV mean gradient and AVA with concomitant
LVOTO.
A second recently published case report described
the approach with ASA before TAVR.7 The patient had
a
continued
presence
of
LVOTO
following
ASA.
Therefore, the decision was made to initiate mava-
camten before TAVR. The patient underwent TAVR a
few
weeks
after
initiating
mavacamten
without
complications.
Finally, it is worth considering that surgical risk
assessment is institution dependent and is inﬂuenced
by multidisciplinary heart team evaluation. At our
institution, factors such as advanced age, frailty,
pulmonary and cardiovascular comorbidities, and
previous surgical history contributed to the classiﬁ-
cation of the majority of our cohort as high surgical
risk. However, different centers may have varying
criteria for surgical risk stratiﬁcation, which could
inﬂuence decision making in managing concomitant
LVOTO and AS. Our approach—using ASA and CMI
therapy before considering AV replacement—repre-
sents 1 of several available strategies for treating
these
complex
patients,
and
further
studies
are
needed to reﬁne patient selection and sequencing of
interventions.
CONCLUSIONS
Our approach for treating oHCM with concomitant AS
would be ASA and potentially CMI therapy. Serial
evaluation of LVOT gradients and repeat symptom
assessment are necessary to determine the degree of
ongoing AS. If symptoms persist following ASA, and
the AV gradients are consistent with severe AS, then
AVR (SAVR/TAVR) could be considered on the basis of
surgical risk. Last, use of CMI therapy before TAVR in
