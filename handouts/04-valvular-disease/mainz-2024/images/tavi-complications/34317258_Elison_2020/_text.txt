CASE REPORT
CLINICAL CASE
TandemHeart-Assisted Balloon Aortic
Valvuloplasty for Acute Transcatheter
Aortic Valve Replacement Thrombosis
David Elison, MD, James McCabe, MD
ABSTRACT
The patient in this report had a catastrophic case of transcatheter aortic valve replacement–related valve thrombosis
manifesting within 1 month of valve implantation that was complicated by acute heart failure and cardiogenic shock.
Anticoagulation and thrombolytic agents were not successful, and the patient was treated with TandemHeart
(Cardiac Assist, Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania)–supported balloon aortic valvuloplasty and cerebral embolic protection.

HISTORY OF PRESENTATION
A 64-year-old man with no medical history pre-
sented with progressive heart failure symptoms. A
transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE) revealed severe
systolic
dysfunction
and
critical
low-ﬂow,
low
gradient
aortic
stenosis.
Coronary
angiography
revealed non obstructive disease in the left system
and a chronic total occlusion of the right coronary
artery. After volume optimization and dental work,
he
underwent
uncomplicated
transfemoral
trans-
catheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) with a
26-mm Sapien 3 valve (Edwards Lifesciences, Irvine,
California) with excellent valve expansion and no
paravalvular regurgitation. The post-procedure mean
gradient was 12 mm Hg. He was discharged on
aspirin, clopidogrel, metoprolol, and diuretic agents.
He presented again 3 weeks later with recurrent
heart failure and cardiogenic shock, and he had
reportedly
been
nonadherent
to
his
medical
regimen.
DIFFERENTIAL DIAGNOSIS
The differential diagnosis included routine decom-
pensated heart failure and ﬂuid overload, acute cor-
onary syndrome or coronary obstruction from the
leaﬂets of the bioprosthesis, or acute change in TAVR
hemodynamics.
LEARNING OBJECTIVES
 Understand additional management strate-
gies for patients with bioprosthetic valve
thrombosis not responding to medical ther-
apy who are not candidates for surgery.
 Review mechanical circulatory support op-
tions in the setting of critical bioprosthetic
aortic valve thrombosis.
 Review the available data regarding anti-
platelet and anticoagulation strategies post-
TAVR.

INVESTIGATIONS
Repeat TTE demonstrated persistent cardio-
myopathy,
with
diminished
bioprosthetic
leaﬂet motion and peak antegrade velocity of
4.1 m/s, mean gradient 42 mm Hg. Coronary
angiogram demonstrated no acute plaque
rupture and no evidence of TAVR leaﬂet
coronary obstruction. Out of concern for
TAVR valve dysfunction, a computed to-
mography angiogram was obtained, which
revealed severely thickened valve leaﬂets,
consistent with bioprosthetic valve throm-
bosis (Figure 1). Right-sided heart catheteri-
zation
was
performed,
with
pulmonary
capillary wedge pressure 31 mm Hg and cardiac index
1.3 by Fick. Diuretic and heparin infusions were
started, and he received 3 boluses of 25 mg of tissue
plasminogen activator over a 10-day period without
clinical improvement. There was clinical agreement
that he was not a candidate for emergency surgical
valve replacement, and because of progressive hy-
potension
he
was
transferred
to
a
high-volume
center.
MANAGEMENT
On arrival, he remained in shock, requiring inotropic
support and vasopressor agents. The decision was
made to pursue mechanical circulatory support to
support valve intervention. After discussion, he was
taken for TandemHeart (Cardiac Assist, Pittsburgh,
Pennsylvania) insertion with stabilization in his he-
modynamics. It was believed that extracorporeal
membrane oxygenation support was not needed,
given
his
normal
right
ventricular
function
and
acceptable respiratory parameters, and passing the
thrombosed valve to place percutaneous intraven-
tricular support was high risk. Next, with a cerebral
embolic protection (CEP) device in place, he under-
went transesophageal echocardiogram (TEE)–guided
balloon aortic valvuloplasty (BAV) with a 26-mm True
(Bard Peripheral Vascular, Tempe, Arizona) balloon
over a 0.035-inch Conﬁda (Medtronic, Minneapolis,
Minnesota)
wire,
which
resulted
in
immediate
improvement in leaﬂet mobility (Videos 1, 2, and 3).
TEE subsequently revealed several large left ventric-
ular thrombi. A large clot burden was captured in
both baskets of the CEP, removed, and sent for
pathological examination, which conﬁrmed thrombus
(Figure 2). The patient’s support was weaned by post-
operative day (POD) 1, and he was extubated. Mag-
netic
resonance
imaging
of
the
brain
revealed
an acute versus subacute small, multifocal cerebro-
vascular accident, although the patient was without
clinically signiﬁcant neurological deﬁcit. He was dis-
charged on POD 19 following medical optimization on
warfarin.
DISCUSSION
Our patient illustrates a catastrophic case of TAVR
thrombosis, generally considered a rare clinical en-
tity, with an incidence of approximately 0.6% in
registry studies (1). The 2017 American College of
Cardiology and American Heart Association guide-
lines recommend urgent surgery, in addition to anti-
coagulation and thrombolytic therapy, in the setting
of New York Heart Association functional class III to
IV symptoms and bioprosthetic valve thrombosis (2).
However, in a patient with a prohibitive surgical risk,
BAV may be an option. Regarding prevention, dual
antiplatelet therapy is guideline recommended for
6 months following valve implantation, although this
is based on the original protocol used in the balloon-
expandable TAVR trials, and it has not been inde-
pendently
validated
(2).
Anticoagulation
can
be
considered, in the absence of a separate indication, in
patients considered at low risk for bleeding. The
recommendation is extrapolated from data in surgical
aortic
valve
replacement
(SAVR)–treated
patients
whose 3-month stroke risk was reduced by anti-
coagulation therapy following valve implantation (2).

FOLLOW-UP
At the time of this writing, the patient had most
recently been seen near his home about 2 months
earlier and was doing well. He was at home after an
extended stay in a skilled nursing facility, and his
medical regimen is slowly being optimized as an
outpatient. He has not been hospitalized again.
CONCLUSIONS
Although clinically signiﬁcant TAVR thrombosis is
uncommon, this patient’s low-ﬂow state, medica-
tion nonadherence, and perhaps other causes (e.g.,
undiagnosed
malignant
disease,
hypercoagulable
state) likely contributed to his severe and sudden
thrombosis. Clear understanding of the best pre-
ventive antiplatelet or anticoagulant regimen and
clinical management of this rare condition remain
elusive.
